What the heat looks like

Illustrative thermal view of an IR lamp treatment on the lower back.

  • The warm zone sits right under the lamp: IR is absorbed at the skin surface (the lecture gives 1–10 mm).
  • Grotthuss–Draper: energy absorbed at the surface is not left for deeper tissue, so IR is a superficial heating agent.
  • The surface warming widens skin blood vessels, which is why blood flow, metabolism and healing increase.
  • Setup shown: patient lying prone and draped, only the lumbar area exposed, goggles on the patient, therapist checking the skin.
  • Check heat sensation first. Skin that cannot feel heat cannot warn you.

  • Longer wavelength means lower photon energy, not higher.
  • Depth depends on how strongly tissue absorbs and scatters that wavelength, not on energy alone.
  • UV: absorbed by proteins and DNA in the outer skin. Visible: melanin and blood absorb. About 600–1300 nm is the “optical window”, absorbed least, so it goes deepest.
  • Beyond about 1400 nm, water absorbs strongly: IR-B and IR-C stay in the surface layers.

Curve is an approximate textbook trend, not clinical data.

Not enough, just right, too much

Arndt–Schultz principle: below a threshold nothing happens, in the window the tissue responds, above it the tissue is damaged.

Why a hot lamp glows infrared

Any warm body emits infrared. Hotter bodies peak at shorter wavelengths (Wien’s law: λ = 2898 ÷ T µm).
